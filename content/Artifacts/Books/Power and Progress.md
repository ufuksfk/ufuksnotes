## Goodreads comments

Probably the authors should go read _How the World Became Rich_, a book with well-argued and well-referenced modern research. The authors need not agree but without better support of their positions (without SOME support of their positions) there's really no justification for accepting their positions.

Examples cited in the book are many and varied, ranging from the inequality engendered by medieval ship designs, the poor working conditions of textile factories during the industrial revolution, the slavery intensified by the innovation of the cotton gin, and the creation of artificial fertilisers that led to the design of chemical weapons.

Power and Progress is ultimately less a book of new economic analysis than a quasi-manifesto and policy prescription for change in how technology is governed. Acemoglu and Johnson identify three broad and arguably statist approaches that are needed to ensure the benefits of tech innovations are shared widely and harness the spirit of previous progressive movements.

These approaches include the imperative to: i) change the narrative so society is no longer 'mesmerised by tech billionaires and their agenda'; ii) cultivate ‘countervailing powers' to keep power dynamics in the hands of the many not the few; and iii) pursue progressive policy solutions, which could involve a range of economic incentives (subsidies for socially beneficial tech, incentives for human capital investment to empower workers and wealth tax reforms).
