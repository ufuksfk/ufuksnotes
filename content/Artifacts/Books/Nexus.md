  **From the author of _Sapiens_ comes the groundbreaking story of how information networks have made, and unmade, our world.**  
    
  For the last 100,000 years, we Sapiens have accumulated enormous power. But despite allour discoveries, inventions, and conquests, we now find ourselves in an existential crisis. The world is on the verge of ecological collapse. Misinformation abounds. And we are rushing headlong into the age of AI—a new information network that threatens to annihilate us. For all that we have accomplished, why are we so self-destructive?  
    
  _Nexus_ looks through the long lens of human history to consider how the flow of information has shaped us, and our world. Taking us from the Stone Age, through the canonization of the Bible, early modern witch-hunts, Stalinism, Nazism, and the resurgence of populism today, Yuval Noah Harari asks us to consider the complex relationship between information and truth, bureaucracy and mythology, wisdom and power. He explores how different societies and political systems throughout history have wielded information to achieve their goals, for good and ill. And he addresses the urgent choices we face as non-human intelligence threatens our very existence.  
    
  Information is not the raw material of truth; neither is it a mere weapon. _Nexus_ explores the hopeful middle ground between these extremes, and in doing so, rediscovers our shared humanity.

## Goodreads notes
*In* Nexus, Yuval Noah Harari explores how human networks of cooperation shape our power and decision-making, particularly in the age of artificial intelligence. While the book raises important questions about information systems and institutional design, it frequently stumbles due to its persistent doomerism and heavy ideological bias.  
  
Harari's strongest moments come when discussing scientific institutions and their self-correcting mechanisms. He astutely observes how scientific progress relies on systematic skepticism and error correction, setting it apart from other social institutions. His distinction between intelligence and consciousness in AI discussions is also noteworthy and well-articulated.  
  
However, the book's analysis is often undermined by oversimplification and ideological positioning. Harari frequently presents false dilemmas and relies on extreme examples to make his points. His understanding of technology and algorithms sometimes appears superficial, particularly in discussions about social media and content recommendation systems. The philosophical arguments, especially regarding deontology and utilitarianism, lack rigor and occasionally misrepresent the underlying concepts.  
  
The book's central thesis—that we must build institutions with strong self-correcting mechanisms—is valuable but gets somewhat lost amid the author's consistent pessimism and ideological positioning.
