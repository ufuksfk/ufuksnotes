I think, the story is begun with a guy: [[Joe Macleod]]. An English designer has a master on Interaction design from Royal College of Art. After spending 15 years mostly as a design consultant or design manager, he started to give an attention to absence of interest to end of life of a product.

His last job before this journey was Head of Design at ustwo, which is another award-winning design agency. And a to-do for me is they have an office in Malmö. 

- [ ] So, go visit them, Ufuk.

And it seems he is living in [[Stockholm]], [[Sweden]].

# And End

As he wrote a book to get attention to the problem, that company journey started at [[2018]].

Their clients and references page is promising. [[Spotify]], [[Volvo]], [[Microsoft]], [[Logitech]], [[Electrolux]], [[Fairphone]] and so on.

But as I read their website, I realized it is a 1-man army :) Apparently, he is kind of consulting to companies and giving speeches. Let’s check what they have to educate ourselves.

## Books

He wrote 2 books as well: Ends in [[2017]] and Endineering in [[2021]]. While Endineering has 4.5 star on Goodreads, Ends only has 3.3. And it seems Endineering has no digital version, while newer book Ends is digitally everywhere. I found Ends as an audiobook in Storytel. So, it would be worth to spend 8 hours to that.

## Course

As they have a course for individuals and businesses, it is not free. For the next cohort planned to start May,7 2024 they ask $500 for 3.5 hours of video content. which is not cool :)

In a consumerist era, all the companies spent millions to promote onboarding of a product or basically selling a new product. I am not sure if it is their fault or more systematic fault of capitalism. But it is good to know there are some people and companies swimming against the tide.

So, it is a bit obvious to everyone what is going to happen to products after a couple of years. Lifetime of a product depends, it might be 20 years for a medical instrument or 30 years for a car or just a couple of years for a mobile phone. Especially for some devices in consumer electronics, they are pushing hard to sell a new one every year. It is not the case for some products like wristwatch, a monitor, even a laptop. Even for a smartwatch, we tend to find something for years. All of my friends wearing Garmin watches, they said I bought it over 5 years ago.

Let’s read how Joe describe it:

While he is specifically talking about the consumer lifecycle of digital devices, these questions could apply to almost any consumer experience, says Macleod. He's identified a cycle that deteriorates from the life-​affirming experience of opening the box of your new smartphone to that "feeling of abandonment after your contract is up, drifting around unsupported with obsolete technology". While the onboarding experience "is fantastic, and the set-up wizards will get you going in minutes", by the end that warm fuzzy feeling has transformed into the cold, hard language of metropolitan recycling schemes.

…

Part of the issue is product perception. When consumers buy a smartphone "they know loads about the product. They will know its value, what it can do, who makes it. The physical identity in the purchase transaction has a clear asset structure. We then use the product and become engaged with it. When we offboard, it goes into the recycling process, and it loses all of its identity assets and is reduced to componentry at the end." Not only that, says Macleod, but all the responsibility for what happens at the end of the product lifecycle has now shifted entirely to the consumer.

From And End perspective, they are basically trying to say 2 years of warranty (3 at most for most products) is too short, and we need to have more clear communication channel with customers. Both for a digital service or a real product, we end up with alone trying to decide what to do with that garbage.

A couple of good examples from their website:

An example from their website is Kia, as they come up with 7-year warranty in 2007 for Ceed model. As far as I know, they are still known to have very long warranty compared to others. So, thank you Kia :)

Paying a compensation to passengers for delayed flights. I couldn’t understand how it relates to end of life, but it is a very humane way to solve the problem.

Cancellation experience of any digital product or even gym membership can be a topic of end of life. They tend to have 1 click subscribe button, but dark UX to cancel subscription.

Another example is closing a product. We know Google likes it. Once they decided to close a product (no one normally likes it), can they do better on that experience?

Having a regulated account switch experience in the USA. Thanks to the regulators, they can now switch between banks quite easily. It had a switching cost like registering all the bills again, but AFAIK, it is not a thing anymore.

Right to be forgotten, data portability with GDPR. It is end of life experience of your data :)

We have plastic bottle deposit machines everywhere in [[Sweden]]. It is a good solution for end of life of a plastic.

Online banks giving you information about your digital subscription. As you spent money, it means an income for a bank. But having a clear communication for forgotten subscription is very humane user experience.

Right to repair, which is kind of hot topic these years. Being able to repair an iPhone is crazy hard with their smart chips in every component.
