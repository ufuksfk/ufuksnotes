Janine Benyus, in her book Biomimicry, beautifully describes the similarities between ecological succession and humankind's ongoing maturation into a species that lives in harmony with the rest of the natural world. Ecological succession involves three different kinds of plant species, each with different behavioral characteristics. 
1. Type one species are certain kinds of grasses, herbaceous plants, and annuals, some might call them weeds, that are adept at pioneering otherwise hostile environments. They grow rapidly and take over every inch of ground available. Their energy is focused on extracting nutrients from the soil and rapidly turning them into seeds. These plants are not what you would call complex or collaborative, they live and die in one season, and so synergies with other plants or revitalizing the soil is unnecessary for their
2. Type two species, like perennials and shrubs, have a more sophisticated relationship with the soil they depend upon. Energy is committed to long-term survival in the form of woody pulp and elaborate root systems. They optimize energy flows, and because they're deeply rooted in place, their every function contributes to the health of the soil and thus to other plant species around
3. Type three species, like trees, are even more sophisticated. They grow slower, and as they mature, they create conditions conducive to life. They're truly stewards of their own future. In other words, while type one species extract nutrients from the soil on their way to somewhere else, type three species nurture the soil they live in.
