# Note
 The authors argue that democracy should be viewed as an open communicative culture rather than a specific government type, as it can take many forms.
The technologies used for communication shape the communicative culture and ultimately influence the direction of a democracy.
Media theorists like Marshall McLuhan and Neil Postman emphasize the importance of understanding how mediums change us, not just the content they deliver.
The introduction of new media technologies often disrupts political systems, leading to unpredictable consequences.
The authors argue that democracy is inherently paradoxical, as the open communication that makes it possible can also be used to undermine it.
The authors believe that our ideology is shaped by our technology, and that changes in technology can fundamentally alter our way of thinking and interacting with the world.
The authors argue that the media's role in shaping political discourse is often overlooked, and that the media itself can be a powerful force in shaping political outcomes.
The authors believe that media literacy is crucial for navigating the complexities of the modern communicative culture and understanding the influence of technology on our lives.
