# Note
 The rise of spiritualism, witchcraft, and astrology within secular circles is a noteworthy phenomenon. I will analyze this trend from psychological and sociological perspectives, not from a theological or historical standpoint. My focus is on why these non-institutional belief systems are appealing to secular individuals.

Many seek answers to existential questions: purpose, identity, and how to live. Secularism, derived from the Latin for "worldly," denotes independence from religious institutions and doctrines. Spiritual practices, unlike institutional religions, lack central authorities, prescribed rituals, and canonical texts; they are largely based on personal experience and intuition.

One contributing factor is the inadequacy of materialistic, atheistic thought to provide compelling alternatives to spiritual practices. The competitive nature of neoliberal society fosters an existential crisis, leaving individuals searching for meaning and self-worth, which spiritual beliefs may offer. Another factor is the "superficial knowledge hypothesis," where simplified explanations, like astrology, satisfy a need for meaning without requiring deeper intellectual engagement.

Astrology, for example, lacks scientific validity; its claims are untestable and have been repeatedly disproven. Similarly, the application of quantum physics to spiritual beliefs is often misused; quantum effects operate at a subatomic level, not on a macro scale. The "insufficient knowledge hypothesis" suggests that belief in these narratives stems from a lack of scientific understanding.

However, the appeal of spiritual practices goes beyond mere lack of knowledge. The "metaphysical unease hypothesis" posits that humans have a fundamental biological need for meaning and belonging, leading to the attribution of intentionality to random events. Belief systems fulfill this need, even if their underlying claims are false. This is analogous to understanding the neurochemistry of love without eliminating the experience of love itself.

Spiritual experiences are psychologically real for individuals, even if not objectively true. This raises fundamental questions about the nature of reality and knowledge. The crucial question is whether we, as secular, scientifically-minded individuals, have created alternative narratives that satisfy the human need for meaning.

While not advocating for a "secular religion," I argue that we need to create a secular system that addresses the human need for meaning. The success of Zen Buddhism in the West demonstrates that a non-theistic system can provide meaning and structure. The failure of Western philosophy to create a comparable system stems from its academic focus and the economic constraints on independent philosophical inquiry.

Therefore, the turn to New Age beliefs is not surprising. My aim is not to criticize those who participate in these practices, but to explore the possibility of creating a more grounded, secular narrative that addresses the human need for meaning, using philosophy and aesthetics to fill the existential void.
