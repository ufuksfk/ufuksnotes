# Note
I wrote my book, [[Closing the Loop]], to help designers integrate systems thinking into their everyday work. I'm not a systems thinking expert, but I've found that the [[design thinking]] process has limitations when it comes to designing for individual users. We need a broader lens to design products at scale and solve wicked problems.

Systems thinking helps us understand the complex systems in which our designs operate. It's not just about understanding the end user, but also the broader context, including unintended consequences and the impact on those who may not even be using the product. I differentiate between hard systems thinking, which focuses on complex engineering problems, and soft systems thinking, which focuses on understanding and defining problem spaces.

I believe that designers can expand their thinking in three key areas: wholeness, interconnectedness, and causality. Wholeness means considering the broader context of a design, not just the specific problem being solved. Interconnectedness means understanding how different elements of a system are connected, including regulatory environments and policy. Causality involves understanding the root causes of problems and anticipating potential unintended consequences.

It's important to remember that systems thinking is not about creating perfect solutions, but rather about understanding the complexities of a system and designing interventions that can improve it. It's also important to engage with stakeholders and conduct research to understand the system and its impact.

I believe that systems thinking can complement design thinking and help us create designs that are more ethical and sustainable. It's not about stopping innovation, but about expanding our thinking and making more informed decisions. By understanding the systems in which we operate, we can create designs that have a positive impact on the world.
