# Note
 This lecture covers five international relations theories. Realism, the oldest theory, emphasizes power and the lack of central authority in the international system, leading states to prioritize self-preservation. Human nature is viewed as inherently selfish.

Neorealism focuses on the systemic level, arguing that a bipolar system (like the Cold War) is stable, while a multipolar system is more fluid. Economic strength is as important as military might in realism.

Liberal and idealist theories contrast realism, pointing to a declining trend in warfare and attributing this to factors like representative governments, increased diplomacy through international institutions (like the UN), and international trade. Kant's ideas on these factors are highlighted.

Constructivism argues that the identity of actors and their perceptions of national interest shape international relations. Nuclear weapons, for example, are not inherently problematic; the problem lies in who possesses them. National interests are socially constructed, not objective.

Marxist theories, such as world-systems theory, analyze the international system through a class-based lens, highlighting economic inequalities between core, semi-periphery, and periphery states. This system creates a chain of exploitation, with core states benefiting disproportionately. Feminist theory emphasizes the gender imbalance in power structures and advocates for greater gender equality in international relations.
