# Note
 I recently found plastic buried in my garden, prompting me to explore plastic's climate impact. The recent failure of a UN treaty to limit plastic pollution highlights this often-overlooked connection.

My investigation into plastic's role in climate change began with an interview with Susan Frankel, author of A Toxic Love Story. Frankel's attempt to go plastic-free for a day revealed the pervasive nature of plastic in modern life.

Plastics, polymers made of long molecular chains, were invented in the 1930s and their production exploded during World War II. Initially viewed as a marvel, the perception of plastic shifted as single-use packaging became prevalent and the environmental consequences became apparent.

The production of plastics consumes significant amounts of fossil fuels, contributing to greenhouse gas emissions. Disposal methods, such as burning, further release these gases. Globally, plastics account for 5% of planet-warming gases, roughly double the emissions from aviation.

To understand the disposal side of the equation, I spoke with Liana Hosea, who reported on Malaysia, a major importer of plastic waste. She found massive, open dumps and burning sites, releasing toxic fumes and harming human health. Recycling is ineffective, with only 9% of global plastic being recycled.

The failed UN plastics treaty aimed to reduce plastic production by 40% by 2040. However, differing economic situations and reliance on fossil fuels among various countries hindered progress. While recycling is insufficient, preventing plastic pollution requires systemic changes, focusing on reducing single-use plastics and promoting sustainable production and consumption.
