  - 5) What is retroduction (abduction) and why is it important in scientific reasoning?
  - 6) How does the episode describe the limits of observation and measurement in science?

  - 8) Why is “confirmation bias” considered both a danger and a necessary element in scientific work?
  - 9) How do the guests differentiate between solving a problem in everyday life (e.g., plumbing) and doing science in the lab?
  - 10) What do they say about the boundary between science and non-science, and why are “borderlands” important?

  **Answers**
  - 1) A scientist’s claim to “know” something is not the same as other knowledge claims; it means something different and is always provisional, with an emphasis on being closer to the truth rather than being absolutely right. 
  - 2) Phenomena are repeatable observations (things that occur); theoretical claims attempt to explain those phenomena, but those explanations can be wrong even when the phenomena repeat reliably. 
  - 3) Science is epistemically distinct because its confidence about claims is grounded in methods, evidence, and testable processes, not just in authority or certainty. 
  - 4) Debate is crucial: it helps expose weaknesses, counter biases, and push theories toward closer approximations of truth; over time, this social process improves understanding. 
  - 5) Retroduction (abduction) is reasoning from effect to possible causes; it’s the backward inference scientists use to generate plausible explanations when direct observation of causes is not possible. 
  - 6) Observations are theory-laden and instrument-influenced; we often infer unseen entities (like atoms or dark matter) from data, which means not all knowledge is directly observed. 
  - 7) Popper emphasized falsification (no amount of evidence proves a theory, but a single counterexample can falsify it); Quine argued that background assumptions and theory-laden data mean hypotheses are tested within a web of beliefs, not in isolation. 
  - 8) Confirmation bias is dangerous because it can harden beliefs in the face of contrary data, but it’s also a natural byproduct of problem-solving and part of how scientists pursue understanding; balance comes from healthy debate and diverse viewpoints. 
  - 9) Everyday problem solving (like troubleshooting a leak) is related to but not identical to scientific research; science requires standardized methods, openness to being proven wrong, and a willingness to revise theories, not just solve a one-off problem. 
  - 10) Boundaries between science and non-science are murky, especially in borderlands like cutting-edge theories; understanding methodology, not just topics, is key to distinguishing science from non-science. 
  - 11) Reproducibility and replication should be valued because they test robustness, but the system should reward replication and falsification efforts, not just flashy new discoveries. 
  - 12) They advocate for educating the public and students about how science actually works (not just the idealized version), promoting debate, reducing hyperbole, teaching debiasing judiciously, and aligning incentives to reward robust, reproducible science.
- ## Science is not about being right—it's about getting closer to rightness
  - Science is not about being right; it’s about getting closer to rightness. 
  - It is iterative and self-correcting, with progress coming from testing, refuting, and refining ideas. 
  - It is a social process that benefits from debate, diverse viewpoints, and rigorous scrutiny within a community.
