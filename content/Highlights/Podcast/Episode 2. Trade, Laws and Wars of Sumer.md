# Note
 Mesopotamian trade flourished, with Damkara traders, often working for temples, exchanging goods across vast distances. Temples functioned like early factories, producing goods for trade and acting as banks, advancing commodities to merchants. This system involved tithing profits back to the temple, supporting temple workers.

Temples and palaces provided essential resources like rations and donkeys to merchants, creating a mixed economy blending private and government enterprise. Merchants used personal capital and funds from private backers, typically relatives, highlighting the importance of temple connections for entrepreneurial success. This resembles the relationship between Russian oligarchs and the Communist party.

Early banking involved storing valuables in secure temple or palace locations. These institutions served as depositories for commodities and precious metals, but were vulnerable to looting during inter-city-state warfare. The first known use of interest, termed "mash," emerged naturally from agricultural practices, reflecting the growth of livestock herds.

Loans were initially made by temples and palaces, often for seeds to be repaid after harvest. Later, personal loans between individuals appeared around 2450 BCE. Compound interest is documented on a clay cone from around 2400 BCE, illustrating a conflict between Lagash and Umma over land and back rent calculations.

The Sumerian accounting system, using tokens and clay tablets (bullae), created the world's first written contracts, essentially futures contracts. These contracts, with tokens inside sealed clay balls, provided a verification and reconciliation process, laying the groundwork for modern financial instruments.

The Uruk accounting system tracked debts, which became transferable, acting as early liquid currency. This system, according to Polanyi, aimed to improve redistribution rather than solely facilitating exchange. A tablet from around 2000 BCE shows a 10-year growth model for cattle and their byproducts, demonstrating early financial modeling.

While silver became a measure of value, trade often occurred in kind. Silver's acceptance was based on social values and trust, despite its limited practical uses. Mesopotamia lacked native silver, unlike Greece, making wealth accumulation more challenging. To prevent social unrest from excessive debt, rulers periodically issued clean slate decrees canceling rural debt.

Sumer's advanced mathematical system, possibly originating in the 6th millennium BCE, was crucial for tracking growing populations, land measurement, and taxation. The base-60 system, possibly linked to finger counting, influenced our modern timekeeping (60 seconds, 60 minutes) and the 360-degree circle. An abacus was likely in use by 2700 BCE.

The Sumerians' contributions extended to creating a 360-day year and a place-value system. Later Babylonian tablets advanced mathematics further. Despite their significant contributions, Sumerian achievements are often overlooked in favor of Greek and Egyptian accomplishments in Western education.

The Sumerians' increased productivity led to the development of the state, legal codes, and banking systems. These advancements reduced the risk of starvation and improved the quality of life, although conflicts with neighboring groups, particularly the Gutians, remained a constant threat.

The Gutians, nomadic shepherds, frequently raided Sumerian and Akkadian settlements, leading to a period of decline known as the "mini Dark Age." Their rule was marked by reduced written records, increased famine, and decreased trade. Ur-Nammu's reign brought standardized weights and measures, and the Code of Ur-Nammu, the oldest surviving law code, established legal frameworks for trade and standardized currency. The code's provisions, many of which resemble biblical laws, highlight the Sumerians' influence on later legal systems. The Sumerians' innovations laid the foundation for future Mesopotamian empires and significantly impacted the development of Western civilization.
