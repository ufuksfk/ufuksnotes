# Note
 [[Milton Friedman]] and [[Ayn Rand]] are both individualists who support capitalism as a social and economic system, focusing on individual freedom and skepticism of collectivism.
Friedman's work with Anna Schwartz on the Great Depression led to the development of monetarism, which emphasized the importance of money in the economy and influenced economic policies.
The [[Chicago School of Economics]], a branch of neoclassical economics, emerged as a more empirical and less model-based approach, contrasting with the model-heavy Keynesian economics that advocated for more government intervention in the economy.
Friedman believed that economic freedom was essential for capitalism, focusing on the ability to keep what one has earned, make business decisions, and determine acceptable wages.
Friedman's emphasis on rules, such as the monetary growth rule in monetarism, highlighted the importance of clear and transparent rules in economic systems to ensure fair competition and prevent political corruption.
Friedman's belief that economic freedom could lead to political freedom was challenged by observations in countries like [[China]], where economic freedom existed without corresponding political freedom, leading to a reevaluation of the relationship between economic and political freedoms.
Knight's metaphor of economic life as a game with rules influenced Friedman's thinking on the importance of rules in maintaining the economic system and ensuring fair competition.
Milton Friedman exerted influence behind the scenes during the [[Richard Nixon]] administration, advocating for floating exchange rates and influencing George Shultz to let [[Bretton Woods]] fade away.
Friedman played a key role in advising [[Ronald Reagan]] on implementing policies to combat inflation, influencing the appointment of Paul Volcker and pushing for interest rates to signal a change in government policy.
Friedman would likely see a use case for cryptocurrency but would not support the idea of multiple competing currencies or a stateless currency, as history shows societies converge on one currency and the state eventually becomes involved in the provision of money.
Friedman formed close relationships with like-minded individuals at the University of Chicago, such as Frank Knight, leading to the formation of the Room seven gang where they explored and debated ideas that shaped his thinking.
Ayn Rand's ideas are often not included in lists of great thinkers due to the dislike of her work and the perception of it as shallow, as well as her detachment from intellectual communities and the idealized nature of her insights.
Ayn Rand's views on gender and sexuality are complex, reflecting her struggles to conform to traditional gender norms and her compensatory theories to explain her own experiences.
Ayn Rand's Objectivist theory of sexuality, which ties sexual desires to one's highest values and rationality, is considered unconvincing and contradictory by many.
Ayn Rand's controversial rape scene in "The Fountainhead" may have been included to be provocative and appeal to readers, drawing on tropes from the romance genre and reflecting her background in screenwriting.
Ideas are powerful forces that shape societies and individuals, often leading to significant historical events and consequences.
Intellectual historians like Jennifer Burns study the evolution of ideas in their historical context, exploring how they resonate with people and form communities.
Ideas can be complex and multifaceted, reflecting the struggles and experiences of their creators, such as Ayn Rand's Objectivist theory of sexuality.
The pursuit of peace in times of conflict requires empathy, understanding, and a willingness to negotiate and compromise, even with leaders who may be perceived as adversaries.
