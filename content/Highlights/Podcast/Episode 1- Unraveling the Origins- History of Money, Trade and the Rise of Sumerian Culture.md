# Note
 My podcast explores the history of money, banking, and trade, aiming to illuminate our current economic and political landscape by examining past developments. I initially planned to focus on American finance, but my research led me to Sumerian Mesopotamia, the "cradle of civilization," as its economic systems laid the groundwork for modern society. Our education system neglects this crucial history, so I'm filling that gap.

The last ice age ended around 15,000 years ago, leading to agricultural domestication and the rise of settlements. In Sumer (modern-day southern Iraq), cities like Uruk and Ur emerged around 3500 BCE, reaching significant populations. Sumer lacked resources, necessitating extensive trade with Anatolia, the Levant, the Indus Valley, and Eastern Arabia.

Sumer's rich alluvial soil enabled surplus food production, fueling population growth and the rise of cities centered around temples that redistributed goods. This trade was facilitated by the development of advanced irrigation techniques (furrow irrigation) and tools, leading to a 500% increase in farm productivity. This surplus allowed for specialization of labor and the expansion of trade.

The Ubaid period (5500-4000 BCE) saw the initial settlement of Sumer, characterized by distinctive pottery that suggests early trade networks. It was a male-dominated society with arranged marriages and a tolerance for prostitution. The Uruk period (4000-2900 BCE) witnessed a massive increase in agricultural productivity due to improved irrigation and tools, leading to further economic growth and specialization.

This economic expansion necessitated a more sophisticated system for tracking transactions, leading to the invention of cuneiform writing. Initially used for accounting and contracts, cuneiform writing revolutionized record-keeping and enabled more complex economic planning and taxation by the temple and palace. This central planning was crucial for managing resources and ensuring the survival of the city-states.

While private land ownership existed, it was largely marginal compared to the temple and palace's control over most productive land. Land sales were rare and usually occurred under duress, such as military conquest or drought. The Uruk period also saw the importation of slaves and the development of urban planning to manage the growing population and economy.

The Early Dynastic period (2900 BCE onward) saw the rise of dynasties and frequent conflicts between city-states over resources. Around 2400 BCE, a major revolt in Lagash, fueled by widespread debt, led to royal edicts canceling debts and freeing indebted servants. This period also marks the slow emergence of silver as a medium of exchange, used for payments and debt discharge, though coinage wouldn't appear for centuries.

Sargon of Akkad (circa 2334-2279 BCE) conquered Sumer and surrounding regions, creating the first true empire in Mesopotamia. His conquest, while destructive in the short term, led to cultural fusion and the standardization of weights and measures, significantly improving trade efficiency. Sargon also weakened the priesthood's power, redistributing wealth and tying his rule to the divine.

The Akkadian Empire's success relied on efficient long-distance trade, facilitated by the invention of maps around 2300 BCE, detailing trade routes and aiding military campaigns. The rarity and value of tin, crucial for bronze production, drove trade across vast distances, including to Afghanistan and potentially the British Isles (though unknown at the time). Sargon's reign, though relatively short-lived, established a benchmark for future Mesopotamian rulers and significantly impacted the development of trade and civilization.
