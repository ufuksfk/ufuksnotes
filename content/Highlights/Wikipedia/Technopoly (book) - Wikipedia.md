[[Technopoly (book)]] wikipedia page
## Highlights
- He defines a technopoly as a society in which technology is deified, meaning “the culture seeks its authorisation in technology, finds its satisfactions in technology, and takes its orders from technology”. It is characterised by a surplus of information generated by technology, which technological tools are in turn employed to cope with, in order to provide direction and purpose for society and individuals.
- Postman considers technopoly to be the most recent of three kinds of cultures distinguished by shifts in their attitude towards technology – tool-using cultures, technocracies, and technopolies.
- Each, he says, is produced by the emergence of new technologies that "compete with old ones…mostly for dominance of their worldviews".
- technocracy, rather than existing in harmony with a theocratic world-view, tools are central to the "thought-world" of the culture.
- Postman cites the example of the [[telescope]]  destroying the [Judeo-Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judeo-Christian) belief that the Earth is the centre of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), bringing about a "collapse…of the moral centre of gravity in the West".
- Postman characterises a technocracy as compelled by the "impulse to invent",[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-6) an ideology first advocated by [Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon) in the early 17th Century.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-7) He believed that human beings could acquire knowledge about the natural world and use it to "improve the lot of mankind",[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-T&O_13-8) which led to the idea of invention for its own sake and the idea of progress.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-9) According to Postman, this thinking became widespread in Europe from the late 18th Century.
- A technopoly is founded on the belief that technique is superior to lax, ambiguous and complex human thinking and judgement, in keeping with one of [Frederick W. Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor)’s ‘Principles of scientific management’.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-15) It values efficiency, precision, and objectivity.
- Postman attributes the origins of technopoly to ‘[scientism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientism)’, the belief held by early social scientists including [Auguste Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte) that the practices of natural and social science would reveal the truth of human behaviour and provide "an empirical source of [moral authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_authority)".
- [Telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphy) and [photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography), he states, redefined information from something that was sought out to solve particular problems to a commodity that is potentially irrelevant to the receiver.
- Postman echoes [Jean Baudrillard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Baudrillard) in this view, who theorises that "technique as a medium quashes … the ‘message’ of the product (its use value)", since a symbol's "social finality gets lost in seriality".
- has also been argued[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-34) that technologies are shaped by social factors more so than by their inherent properties. Star suggests that Postman neglects to account for the "actual development, adaptation and regulation of technology".
- Postman's characterisation of technology as an ideological being has also been criticised.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-37) He refers to the "god" of technopolists speaking of "efficiency, precision, objectivity", and hence eliminating the notions of sin and evil which exist in a separate "moral universe".[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technopoly#cite_note-P_90-16) [Stuart Weir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Weir) argues that technologies are "not ideological beings that take…near-anthropomorphic control of people’s loves, beliefs and aspirations". He in fact suggests that new technologies have had remarkably little effect on pre-existing human beliefs.
