## Highlights
- But does this societal push towards hyper-specialization genuinely hold up under scrutiny? This dilemma was examined by celebrated psychologists [[Daniel Kahneman]] and Gary Klein, whose research published in 2009 uncovers some surprising findings. While certain professions, like firefighting, benefit extensively from specialized experience — allowing veterans to read patterns in flames and react almost automatically — such clear advantages are not universally applicable.
- Kahneman's investigation of the Israeli Defence Forces’ officers provides a stark contrast. Here, the evaluators’ repeated attempts to predict an officer's potential based on standard metrics were no more accurate than random guesses. Even more tellingly, the officers’ evaluative skills didn't improve with experience — showcasing a blatant disconnect between the amount of experience in a field and the quality of performance within it.
- Take Yo-Yo Ma, the illustrious cellist, whose foray into music wasn’t a straight march to the cello. He sampled the violin and piano before settling into the deep, rich tones of the cello, his true love in the symphony of instruments. Similarly, a study by music psychologist John Sloboda at a British boarding school revealed that students who had experimented with three different instruments were more likely to excel than their counterparts who had narrowed down their focus from an earlier age.
- But the million-dollar question lingered: why were IQ scores increasing worldwide? Alexander Luria's work as a Russian psychologist in the early years of the Soviet Union's transformation shines a light on the answer. With societal progress transforming ancient, isolated villages into hubs of collective farming and industrial endeavors, Luria observed a fascinating shift in people's cognitive abilities.
- In his experiments, Luria found that in the most traditional villages, residents struggled with the concept of grouping items based on abstract categories, like color. When confronted with a pile of varying shades of wool, they saw each piece as distinct and irreplaceable — grouping them was nonsensical to their practical experience-driven minds.
- Similarly, when asked to identify which figure did not belong in a group of images with three adults and one child, a villager named Rakmat envisioned the boy not in abstract isolation, but as part of a collective reality, working alongside the adults. For him, and many like him, abstract categorization was subordinate to the tangible roles they played in everyday life.
- The practice of "spacing" is one such powerful learning tool that embodies desirable difficulties. By incorporating intervals between initial learning and subsequent review sessions, we can significantly improve memory retention. A 1987 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology demonstrated this by testing two groups of Spanish students—the spacing between initial learning and subsequent testing proved that those given time to forget and relearn retained over twice as much vocabulary.
- Even the seemingly insignificant act of spacing by mere seconds can have an impact. When Iowa State University researchers first challenged a group with immediate recall and another after a brief mathematical distraction, the immediate recall group shone. However, later on, when memories had to traverse the longer pathway from short-term to long-term storage, the group that initially struggled remembered more.
- Thus, if you find yourself tempted by the siren call of quick learning and easy progress, remember the tortoise's slow and steady approach. Embrace the struggle, persevere through the challenges, and cling to the 'desirable difficulties.' It's the effortful journey in learning, not the swift ascent, that carves the deepest grooves into the bedrock of our memory.
- This peculiarity isn't confined to the medical field. Businesses, too, face challenges when experts look at situations through the narrow lens of their own experience. This was demonstrated in a study by Dan Lovallo, a professor from the University of Sydney. Private equity investors were asked to scrutinize and predict returns on businesses they intended to invest in. Yet when they briefly considered similar, albeit unrelated, projects, their expected returns on their original investments were suddenly deemed overly optimistic, prompting a hefty recalibration of their projections.
- Breaking free from the confines of our specialized knowledge allows for more nuanced and informed perspectives, sparing us from the pitfalls of a myopic viewpoint.
- Their assumptions, however, crumbled like the pages of an old comic book. They found that the richness of a creator's work was not tied to the quantity of comics they produced nor to the financial muscle behind them. Instead, it was the diversity of their creative endeavors—the number of different genres from comedy to crime and fantasy to non-fiction—that fueled commercial triumph.
- This correlation between breadth and accomplishment extends far beyond the artistic sphere. Consider [[Andy Ouderkirk]], a standout inventor at [[3M]] who was bestowed with the title of Innovator of the Year in [[2013]]. With 170 patents to his name, Ouderkirk delved into the anatomy of successful, inventive teams. His discovery echoed the comic book study's outcome: the innovators making waves and garnering accolades like 3M's Carlton Award were not mere specialists. They were polymaths, individuals who had a primary focus but also boasted wide-ranging expertise across multiple fields.
- Imagine a world-class ensemble of 284 experts, esteemed for their knowledge, predicting the future over the span of two decades. Now picture that their forecasts are no more accurate than if they were tossing darts blindfolded. This is not a fictional scenario, but the finding of forecasting authority Philip Tetlock, who, during the tense years of the Cold War, put expert predictions to the test only to uncover their startling unreliability.
- Advanced degrees, years steeped in a specialized field, or privileged access to sensitive data—none of these factors translated into predictive prowess. When these experts deemed an event impossible, it defied their expectations 15 percent of the time. The occurrences they guaranteed with unwavering certainty faltered one in four times. Moreover, Tetlock unearthed an unsettling inverse relationship: the more frequently an expert appeared on television, the less reliable their forecasts turned out to be.
