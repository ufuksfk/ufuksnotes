## Highlights
- In [[2012]], when [[Facebook]] chief executive [[Mark Zuckerberg]] cut a $1 billion check to [buy the photo-sharing app Instagram](https://archive.ph/o/QEGOU/https://archive.nytimes.com/dealbook.nytimes.com/2012/04/09/facebook-buys-instagram-for-1-billion/), most people thought he had lost his marbles.
- Two years later, Mr. Zuckerberg opened his wallet again when [Facebook agreed to buy WhatsApp](https://archive.ph/o/QEGOU/https://archive.nytimes.com/dealbook.nytimes.com/2014/02/19/facebook-to-buy-messaging-start-up/) for $19 billion. Many Americans had never heard of the messaging app, which was popular internationally but was not well known in the United States.
- [[Kevin Systrom]], Instagram’s six-foot-five co-founder, was then 28. He often walked laps around the wood-and-iron swing set in South Park Commons while taking calls or talking product ideas with employees. [[Jack Dorsey]], a co-founder of Twitter who identified as more of an art kid than a techie, also hung out at the same South Park playground and mused to friends about ideas that eventually became his social media app.
- [[WhatsApp]], which was growing quickly internationally, was a text messaging app without a business model. And clones of these apps were plentiful, such as Color, Flickr and VSCO in photo sharing, and Kik, Skype and Viber in messaging.
- What if other competitors had created superior photo-sharing apps that could have thrived if Facebook hadn’t used Instagram to crush them? And what if Facebook had screwed up both deals, or could not keep up with competing apps and fell behind even after buying Instagram and WhatsApp?
