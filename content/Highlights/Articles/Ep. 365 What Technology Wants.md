## Highlights
- **Additive Vs Extractive Technologies**
  - Some technologies purely add value, like an old landline that makes talking easier. 
  - Others are extractive, designed to capture attention and monetize your time and data.
  Transcript:
  Cal Newport
  So Jesse, I don't know if you noticed this, but several people sent us this same Substack post recently. It had an arresting photo. I'm going to put this up on the screen for people who are watching instead of just listening. You see in this photo something you don't normally see. The young kid laying on the couch talking on an old-fashioned hardwired landline, the whole curly wire and everything. Here's what the author of this essay, Priscilla Harvey, wrote. I bought my kids an old-fashioned phone to keep smartphones out of their hands while still letting them chat with friends. But it has turned into the sweetest, most unexpected surprise, my son's new daily conversation with his grandmother's. This is how I keep finding him stretched out on the couch, phone pressed to his ear, intently focused and listening to the matriarchs of our family. I hear laughter and sense a deeper connection blossoming in these conversations. There's no scrolling, distractions, no comparisons, no dopamine hits the chase. Instead, he is just listening to stories, asking questions, and having the comfort of knowing someone who loves him is listening on the other end of the line. Somehow what was once ordinary in my childhood feels sacred today. But clearly Harvey wrote this article to make a point about kids and phones. That's an important point I want to talk about often on this show. But embedded in here is an even deeper idea, an idea that I think is fundamental to our broken relationship with technology. I want you to think about the two phones that are referenced in this article. The telephone in that original hard plastic curly wired form is an example of what we might call an additive technology because its goal is to take something you value like talking to People you know and make this activity easier and more accessible in your life. You want to talk to your grandmother? Type in some numbers. There's her voice clear in your ear as if you were right next door. So the phone in its original form seeks strictly to add value to your life. Now let's compare this to something modern like the Instagram app you might be using on a smartphone. Now here the value proposition becomes suddenly more muddled. I mean there might be aspects of this app you enjoy. It can be distracting or occasionally you find out something interesting going on with your friend, but those joys come with endless sorrows as well. The scrolling can become addictive. The content can devolve into one of those digital slurries, equal parts mind-nummy and anxiety-producing. So unlike the straightforward benefits of something like an old-fashioned landline, become clear that a tool like Instagram on a new smartphone doesn't have your best interest as Its primary goal. It's using you. It's making itself just compelling enough that you'll pick it up, at which point it can monetize every last ounce of your time and your data. Because of this, I would call the modern smartphone with Instagram an extractive technology as it seeks to extract value from you instead of trying to provide it.
