## Highlights
- **Kant's Space and Time**
  - Kant argues space and time don't exist objectively in the world.
  - Instead, humans inherently recognize space and time, enabling experiences through the mind's capacity to intuit them.
  Transcript:
  David Guignion
  So some of these that he lays out are like humanity's capacity and all living things capacity to witness things within space and time. That is space and time for Kant to do not exist out there in the world. They do not just exist and then we are born into them and then have to learn about space and time. We instead house that very recognition of those things within us. And it is by virtue of our mind's capacity to intuit them in coordination with our bodies occupying space
- **Hegel's Disagreement with Kant**
  - Hegel fundamentally disagrees with Kant's approach to finding the self through analyzing the external world and metaphysics. 
  - Hegel's philosophy seems like pre-Kantian metaphysics, using concepts like spirit and God, and idealizing Christianity.
  Transcript:
  David Guignion
  And this is the point where Hegel totally disagrees. Hegel disagrees so hard that you wouldn't even think that he even read Kant because he's just going so far afield from Kant. That is, if you were to read Kant, you were to read the first critique, the prolegomena, the second, third critique, everything, and then you were to go and read the phenomenology of Spirit, you'd sit there being like, isn't this all just like pre-Kantian metaphysics?
- **Hegel's Critique of Kant**
  - Hegel disagrees with Kant's approach of seeking the self in external experience. 
  - Hegel uses concepts like spirit and God, which Kant argues are unknowable.
  Transcript:

  And this is the point where Hegel totally disagrees. Hegel disagrees so hard that you wouldn't even think that he even read Kant because he's just going so far afield from Kant. That is, if you were to read Kant, you were to read the first critique, the prolegomena, the second, third critique, everything, and then you were to go and read the phenomenology of Spirit, you'd sit there being like, isn't this all just like pre-Kantian metaphysics? Hegel is using words like spirit, he's talking about God, he's just naively lionizing Christianity, which would all seem like hollow concepts. Because Kant's big thing was to say that we can't know these things. Like we can't know what a spirit is. We can't know what God is. We can maybe find evidence of their existence within experience.
- **Growth Through Difference**
  - Hegel says that personal growth comes from confronting things different from you. 
  - When you're challenged by the unfamiliar, you can adapt to better fit how you experience the world.
  Transcript:
  David Guignion
  But for Hegel, he's like, when you actually live in the world, you grow when you confront things that are different from you. You find yourself challenged in whatever you constitute yourself to be or believe yourself to be, in encountering things that are unfamiliar to you, not in things that are familiar. And so Hegel's like, well, how deep does this go? And he says that actually it's kind of baked into our ontology. That is, it is baked into our very being. And what he means is to say that in the moment in which we are born, we have been pulled from kind of pool of non-existence into existence, Hegel holds on to the idea that there is something That separates you from the shell that you occupy. And indeed, many people look at their shell, their bodies, and they're like, that's not really me, and they would opt to change it to adapt to something that better fits how they experience The world. But regardless of like any individual person's choices, Hegel is on to a more universal point. And that is that we first come into the world not by recognizing something and saying, oh, that's an expression of my mind out there. You first encounter the world by coming into contact with difference. You come into contact with bodies that are not your own. Eventually, if we follow Lacan, at some point we become aware of ourselves as an autonomous being, maybe around three or four years old, whenever Lacan says that
