## Highlights
- [[Social media]] platforms have been implicated in conflicts of all scales, from [urban gun violence](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2023/09/jarell-jackson-shahjahan-mccaskill-killed-philadelphia-social-media/674760/) to the [storming of the U.S. Capitol building](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2023/01/17/jan6-committee-report-social-media/) on January 6 and [civil war in South Sudan](https://documents-dds-ny.un.org/doc/UNDOC/GEN/N16/350/68/PDF/N1635068.pdf?OpenElement). Scientifically, it is [difficult to tell](https://theconversation.com/misinformation-why-it-may-not-necessarily-lead-to-bad-behaviour-199123) how much social media can be blamed for one-off incidents.
- But what if we redesigned social media to bridge divides? “[Bridging-based ranking](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/bridging-based-ranking)” is an alternative kind of algorithm for ranking content in social media feeds that explicitly aims to build mutual understanding and trust across differing perspectives.
- The core logic of bridging-based ranking has already been used on [Facebook](https://bridging.systems/facebook-papers/) and [X](https://communitynotes.twitter.com/guide/en/about/introduction) (formerly known as Twitter), albeit not in the main feed. It is also used in [Polis](https://pol.is/home), an online platform for collecting public input, used by several governments to inform policymaking on polarized topics.
- There are many open questions, but evidence from existing uses of bridging-based ranking suggests that changes to algorithms may [reduce partisan animosity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13912) and [improve the quality and inclusiveness](https://bridging.systems/facebook-papers/) of online interactions.
- People are increasingly looking for alternative algorithms. Regulators [in the EU](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/25/quiet-qutting-ai/) and new platforms [such as Bluesky](https://blueskyweb.xyz/blog/3-30-2023-algorithmic-choice) are giving users choice regarding which algorithm determines what they see, and recent [large-scale experiments on Facebook](https://www.science.org/content/article/does-social-media-polarize-voters-unprecedented-experiments-facebook-users-reveal) have tested different options.
- [Bridging-based ranking](https://bridging.systems/) uses a different set of signals to determine which content gets ranked highly. One approach is to increase the rank of content that receives positive feedback from people who normally disagree. This creates an incentive for content producers to be mindful of how their content will land with “the other side.”

- Among the [internal Facebook documents](https://bridging.systems/facebook-papers/) leaked by whistleblower Frances Haugen in 2021, there is evidence that Facebook tested this approach for ranking comments.
- A similar strategy is used in [Community Notes](https://communitynotes.twitter.com/guide/en/about/introduction), a crowd-sourced fact-checking feature on X, to identify notes that are helpful to people on both sides of politics.
- Conflict is an important part of society, and in many cases, a key driver of [political and social change](https://www.jstor.org/stable/586859). The goal of bridging is not to eliminate conflict or disagreement, but to promote constructive forms of conflict.
- This is known as [conflict transformation](https://www.beyondintractability.org/essay/transformation). Professional mediators, facilitators and “peacebuilders”, who work with opposing groups, have a detailed understanding of [how conflicts escalate](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/the-algorithmic-management-of-polarization-and-violence-on-social-media). They also know how to structure communication between opposing groups in ways that build mutual understanding and trust.
- The problem is, platforms normally get cold feet and cancel experiments before they can observe such long-term benefits. Evidence we *do* have from [leaked Facebook papers](https://bridging.systems/facebook-papers/) suggests that incorporating bridging [improves the user experience](https://youtu.be/ePh_DVi3dMM).
- Bridging-based ranking might also have benefits beyond engagement. By reducing [toxicity](https://lukethorburn.com/files/BridgingBasedRanking-PluralitySpringSymposium.pdf#page=13) and content that [violates community guidelines](https://bridging.systems/facebook-papers/), it would likely reduce the need for costly content moderation.
- Important questions around bridging-based ranking remain, and we set out many of these in a [recent paper](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/bridging-systems) published with the Knight First Amendment Institute, which publishes original scholarship and policy papers relating to the defence of freedoms of speech and the press in the digital age.
